# Nail Shapes and Lengths: A Practical Guide

> Compare nine nail shapes, from round to coffin, and four lengths. Choose a shape for your hands and daily routine, with consultation tips for nail techs.

HTML version of this page: https://nailfolio.app/en/blog/nail-shapes-and-lengths/ | Published: 2026-10-15 | Category: How-to Guides

**Nail shape describes the outline; nail length describes how far the nail extends beyond the fingertip.** Choose them together. A rounded outline can work on a very short nail, while a recognizable almond or coffin usually needs more free edge to form its taper.

Names and salon conventions vary, so use a reference photo to compare the outline and length you want. There is no shape that every hand should have. Your preference, available length and daily routine are better starting points than a rule about finger size.

## Nail shapes at a glance

These are visual descriptions, not construction instructions. Names vary between salons, especially for softer versions of square and almond. A reference photo helps settle the outline you mean.

| Shape | How to recognize it | Useful starting length | Main consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Square | Straight sides and a flat tip with defined corners | Short or medium | Corners remain noticeable |
| Squoval | A mostly straight tip with rounded corners | Short or medium | Keeps width without sharp corners |
| Oval | Curved sides flowing into a rounded tip | Short with some free edge, or medium | Softer and less tapered than almond |
| Round | A compact curve following the fingertip | Short | Little projecting length |
| Almond | Tapered sides and a softly pointed tip | Medium or long | Needs room for the taper |
| Coffin or ballerina | Tapered sides ending in a flat tip | Medium or long | Flat tip must remain visible |
| Stiletto | A pronounced taper ending in a sharp point | Long or XL | Point affects everyday handling |
| Lipstick | An intentionally slanted tip | Medium or long | Direction and angle need agreement |
| Edge | Angular form with a raised central ridge | Long or XL | Three-dimensional construction matters |

## What the nine shapes actually look like

### Square and squoval

**Square nails** have a straight free edge and visible corners. They suit a crisp outline and give a French tip a clear horizontal finish. A short square nail and a long square extension share a name, but they do not feel the same in use.

**Squoval nails** combine a square outline with softened corners. The tip stays fairly straight instead of becoming a continuous semicircle. Ask for squoval when you like the width of square but prefer less angular edges. If a reference looks almost round, point out how much straight tip you want to keep.

### Round and oval

**Round nails** follow a compact curve around the fingertip. They are a useful option when you want little free edge and an outline that does not emphasize corners. Round does not mean filing deeply into the sides to force a narrow appearance.

**Oval nails** have a more elongated curve. Compared with round, there is usually more visible length between the fingertip and the rounded end. Compared with almond, the tip is broader and less pointed. On very short nails, the distinction between round and oval may be subtle.

### Almond

**Almond nails** narrow toward a softly pointed end, resembling the outline of an almond. The taper should be visible, but the tip is not the needlelike point of a stiletto. Medium length often gives more room to show the shape than a nearly flush free edge.

An inspiration photo cannot tell you whether your existing natural length is enough. Ask your stylist to show where the taper would begin and whether the result requires an extension. A shorter oval may give you the softer outline you wanted without adding as much length.

### Coffin or ballerina

**Coffin nails**, also called **ballerina nails**, narrow along the sides and finish with a flat tip. The names are often interchangeable, although some stylists use ballerina for a softer version. Agree on the photo rather than assuming a separate universal definition.

The flat end distinguishes coffin from almond. Too little free edge can make the taper hard to see; an exaggerated taper can make the tip much narrower than expected. Check both the tip width and the overall length before the final shape is finished.

### Stiletto

**Stiletto nails** finish in a sharp point. They create a stronger pointed silhouette than almond and usually involve substantial length. A softened almond is not a short stiletto simply because its tip looks slightly pointed.

Consider how the point will interact with clothing, contact lenses, touchscreens and your work. If this is your first pointed set, discuss a shorter alternative and try ordinary hand movements before committing to an XL version.

### Lipstick and edge

**Lipstick nails** have a diagonal tip, like the slanted end of a lipstick bullet. Confirm which way the slope should run on each hand. From one camera angle the shape may look square, so use a straight-on reference as well as a posed photo.

**Edge nails** have an angular outline and a raised central ridge. This is a three-dimensional shape, not just a pointed tip filed flat. Ask whether your stylist offers this construction and request side-view examples. A top-view photo alone cannot show the structure you are agreeing to.

## Nail length guide: short, medium, long and XL

There is no universal millimeter boundary between these labels. Nail bed length changes the overall appearance, and salons may classify the same free edge differently. For a precise agreement, combine the label with a measurement beyond the fingertip or a clear reference.

- **Short:** little free edge, often close to the fingertip. Start by comparing round, squoval and square.
- **Medium:** a visible extension beyond the fingertip with space for a clearer oval, almond or tapered flat tip.
- **Long:** a pronounced free edge that makes the silhouette a major part of the design. Discuss daily handling as well as appearance.
- **XL:** an extra-long result that calls for a deliberate conversation about everyday tasks, construction and upkeep.

Measure the free edge rather than guessing from a cropped photograph. Ask whether the reference shows natural nails or extensions. Also agree whether the finished set will follow the shortest nail or whether a shorter nail needs extending to match the others.

## Matching the shape to your hands and routine

### What is the best nail shape for short fingers?

There is no universally best shape for short fingers. If you want to compare outlines, look at oval, almond and square examples at a similar length and view the whole hand rather than a close crop. You do not need to change your natural proportions to meet a style rule.

### Wide nail beds and reference photos

Use a photo to point out the outline you like, then ask how it would look at your preferred length. Avoid treating a particular nail-bed width as something that needs correction. Your stylist can explain what can be shaped safely on your natural nails.

### Typing, care work, sport and hobbies

Choose against actual tasks: fastening jewelry, handling small objects, wearing gloves, playing an instrument or using a keyboard. If your workplace sets nail requirements, check those before choosing a style. Shorter nails may be easier for your routine, but no shape guarantees that nails will never catch or break.

## For nail techs: turn a shape name into an agreement

Use a reference to confirm three separate things: **outline, free-edge length and the result that is feasible on these nails**. The [consultation before the appointment guide](/en/blog/nail-consultation-before-the-appointment/) explains how to prepare that conversation.

A useful question is: “In this photo, do you like the taper, the flat tip or the length?” Then show a similar set from your own work. Confirm the choice with a plain description such as “medium coffin, narrow sides, flat tip” rather than relying on the photo alone.

When tagging portfolio work, use a consistent short, medium, long and XL system within your gallery. Check the free edge and include a clear view of the finished hand. Avoid tagging the same length differently because a dark color or camera angle makes it appear longer. Keep shape details in the consultation rather than assuming length labels explain them.

## FAQ

### What nail shape is best for short nails?

Round, squoval and square are useful starting points for short nails because they need less free edge to show their outline. The best choice depends on the natural nail, your preferred corners and daily tasks. Almond and coffin usually need more length for a recognizable taper.

### What is the difference between almond and coffin nails?

Almond nails taper to a softly pointed, curved tip. Coffin nails taper along the sides but end with a flat tip. Compare both at the same length, since a long reference beside a short one makes the difference harder to judge.

### Are squoval nails the same as oval nails?

Squoval nails keep a mostly straight tip with rounded corners. Oval nails have a continuous rounded outline without that distinct flat end. Ask your stylist how much straight tip you want to retain, since salon names can overlap.

### How many millimeters are medium nails?

There is no universal millimeter definition of medium nails. Agree on how far the free edge should extend beyond the fingertip and use a reference from your stylist's work. A label alone does not account for different nail beds or salon conventions.

### Can I change from square to almond without extensions?

You may be able to change square nails to almond if there is enough natural free edge to create the taper. Shaping removes material, so the result can be shorter than expected. Ask your stylist to assess the available length and compare a shorter oval before deciding.
